## Why some events become news

Thousands of things happen in Nigeria every day. A newsroom can only report a few of them. So how do editors choose?

They ask a set of questions about each event. Journalists call these questions <Term id="news-values">news values</Term>.

There is no single official list. Researchers have studied news values since the 1960s, and their lists differ. But most newsrooms look for the same basic things. This lesson covers seven of them.

## Seven news values

**Timeliness.** It happened recently, or it is about to happen. A new fuel price announced this morning is news today. In a month, it is old.

**Proximity.** It happens close to your readers. A flood in Lekki matters more to a Lagos newspaper than a similar flood far away. Proximity can also be about connection, not distance. A Nigerian artist winning an award abroad feels close to Nigerian readers.

**Prominence.** It involves well-known people or powerful institutions. If a state governor is in a car accident, it is news. If a private citizen has the same accident, it may not be.

**Impact.** It affects many people, or it affects people deeply. A rise in exam fees for every secondary school student has wide impact.

**Conflict.** There is a disagreement or a struggle. Strikes, court cases, protests and elections all involve conflict.

**Human interest.** It moves people. A story about a woman who teaches children to read in her shop after closing time may not be urgent, but readers care about it.

**Novelty.** It is unusual or surprising. Something that rarely happens gets attention because it is rare.

<Callout type="practice">
Strong stories usually have more than one news value. Imagine a famous musician is arrested at Lagos airport this morning. That story has prominence (a famous person), timeliness (this morning) and proximity (Lagos). It may also have conflict.
</Callout>

## Your audience changes the answer

The same event can be big news for one outlet and small news for another.

Take a Nollywood film that breaks a box office record. An entertainment website may make it the top story. A business newspaper may cover it as a story about money. A politics desk may not cover it at all.

So when you judge a story, always ask: news for whom?

<Callout type="nigeria">
Nigerian audiences are not all the same. A story can matter a lot in Kano and very little in Port Harcourt. Readers of a Yoruba-language station may want different stories from readers of an English-language business paper. Know who you are writing for.
</Callout>

## News values are not the same as ethics

News values explain what gets attention. They do not tell you what is right to publish.

<Callout type="watch">
Conflict and celebrity attract readers. That can push journalists to chase gossip or make a disagreement sound bigger than it is. A story can have strong news values and still be unfair or harmful. You will study how to make those choices in Week 5.
</Callout>

There is also a difference between what interests the public and what is in the <Term id="public-interest">public interest</Term>. A celebrity's new hairstyle may interest many people. A report on how a local government spent its budget is in the public interest, even if fewer people click on it. Good journalism makes the second kind of story interesting too.

## Try it yourself

<Try>
Open a Nigerian news website or app. Pick three stories from today.

1. For each story, write down which news values it shows.
2. Decide which of the three is strongest, and why.
3. Add at least one of them to your news diary, and tick the news values you found.
</Try>
