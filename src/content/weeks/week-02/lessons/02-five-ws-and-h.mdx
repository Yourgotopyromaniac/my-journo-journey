## The six questions

Every news story should answer six questions. Journalists call them <Term id="5ws-and-h">the 5 Ws and H</Term>:

- **Who** is involved?
- **What** happened?
- **When** did it happen?
- **Where** did it happen?
- **Why** did it happen?
- **How** did it happen?

These questions help you in two ways. They tell you what to find out when you report. They also tell you what to put near the top when you write.

## Use them when you report

Before you write, check that you can answer all six questions. If one is missing, you may need to make another phone call or check another document.

Sometimes you cannot answer a question yet. The cause of a fire may not be known for days. Do not guess. Say what is known and what is not.

<Callout type="practice">
If the cause is not known, write it plainly: "Fire officials have not said what caused the fire." Readers trust you more when you are clear about what you do not know.
</Callout>

## Which question comes first?

A news story does not answer the six questions in order. You choose the one that matters most to your reader and start there.

- **What** usually leads. Most of the time, the event is the news.
- **Who** leads when the person is well known. "A former governor has been charged..." works because readers know who that is.
- **When** and **where** almost never lead. They are needed, but they belong later in the sentence.
- **Why** and **how** often come in the second or third paragraph, because they take longer to explain.

<Callout type="watch">
If the person is not well known, do not start with their name. "Chidi Okafor, 34, has..." means nothing to most readers. Start with what they did, then name them later: "A Lagos bus driver who returned ₦1.5 million left on his bus..."
</Callout>
