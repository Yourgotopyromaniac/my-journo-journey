## What a style guide is

Should you write "10" or "ten"? "Mr Adebayo" or "Adebayo"? "N500" or "₦500" or "500 naira"? Different newsrooms answer these questions differently.

A **style guide** sets out one newsroom's rules for spelling, punctuation, numbers, names and other choices. It keeps every story consistent. Sub-editors are usually the people who make sure stories follow it.

When you join a newsroom, ask for its style guide on day one. If there is not a written one, study how the outlet writes and copy it.

## Free guides you can use now

Two major free style guides are online: the **BBC News style guide** and the **Guardian style guide**. Both use British English, which is closer to Nigerian English than American guides.

Some examples from the BBC guide:

- **Numbers:** words for one to nine, digits from 10 upwards. But use digits for measurements and percentages, like 3kg and 4%. Never start a sentence with digits.
- **Dates:** day before month, with no "th": 12 April 2026, not April 12th.
- **Titles:** the BBC does not use Mr, Mrs, Miss or Ms at first or second mention. Professional titles like Dr are used only at first mention.

Until you join a newsroom, choose one style and stick to it. A simple start: words for one to nine; "N" or "naira" used the same way every time; full name first, then surname; dates as day, month, year.

## Headlines

A news headline tells the story in a few words. In many newsrooms, sub-editors write or edit headlines, but reporters should suggest one.

**Be accurate.** Poynter puts it simply: it is fine to make people curious, but "don't mislead or overpromise". A headline that misleads is a broken promise to your reader.

**Start with subject and verb.** Who did what? "Lagos cuts market fees" is clearer than "Market fees: new development".

**Use the present tense.** Headlines often use the present tense for events that have already happened, because it feels immediate: "Fire destroys 40 shops". For future events, they often use "to": "Lagos to cut market fees".

**Use active verbs** where you can.

**Avoid clickbait.** A clickbait headline hides the news to make people click, or promises more than the story gives. Honest headlines can still be interesting.

**Be careful with punctuation.** The Guardian style guide says headlines should never use exclamation marks, and should avoid question marks and quotation marks unless they are essential.

<Callout type="nigeria">
Nigerian newspapers often add attribution after a dash at the end of a headline: "Gunmen abduct six imams in Zamfara - Police". This makes clear the claim comes from someone else.
</Callout>
