## Saying where it came from

<Term id="attribution">Attribution</Term> tells readers who said something or where a fact came from. It lets readers judge how much to trust the information. It also shows you did not make it up.

## "Said" is your best friend

Use **said** most of the time. It is neutral. It reports that someone spoke without suggesting what you think of their words. You can use it again and again without it sounding odd to readers.

<Callout type="practice">
Watch out for verbs like "lamented", "stressed" and "vowed" too. Each one adds a feeling or judgement. Ask yourself: did the person really lament, or did they just say it?
</Callout>

## What needs attribution

- **Opinions always do.** There is no way around it. If it is a view, say whose view it is.
- **Specific claims do.** Numbers, accusations, promises and new information need a clear source.
- **Facts you saw yourself** may not need attribution: "The road was flooded" if you were there.
- **Common knowledge** does not always need a source, such as the date of a public holiday.

When in doubt, attribute.

## Where attribution goes

- **In the lede,** attribution can go at the end so it does not slow down the news: "...no one was hurt, fire officials said."
- **With a quote,** put "she said" after the first sentence of the quote.
- **When the speaker changes,** name the new speaker before their words, so readers are never confused about who is talking.

## Unnamed sources

Sometimes a person will only talk if you do not name them. Newsrooms treat this carefully.

- Reuters says a named source is preferable to an unnamed one "in most circumstances". Unnamed sources should be used only when the information is important and not available on the record.
- The Associated Press only uses anonymous material when it is information, not opinion, and the source has direct knowledge. The story must also explain why the person asked not to be named. Simply writing "a source said" is not allowed.
- Your editor may ask who your source is. At Reuters, a source's agreement is with the organisation, not only the reporter.

Article 4 of the Code of Ethics for Nigerian Journalists says a journalist "should not disclose the source of information obtained in confidence". If you promise to protect someone, keep that promise. So think carefully before you make it, and agree with your editor how unnamed sources will be handled.

## Attribution is not a legal shield

Writing "according to a source" does not protect you if the claim is false and damaging. The News Manual says attributing a statement to someone is no defence against a defamation claim. Attribution tells readers where information came from. Checking it is still your job.
