## Beyond the lede

Last week you learned to write a lede and to put facts in inverted pyramid order. This week you build the rest of the story around them.

Most hard news stories have the same parts, in roughly the same order.

## The second paragraph

The lede cannot say everything. The second paragraph does the next most important job. The News Manual says the sentences below the intro give detail that "supports the facts or opinions given in the intro".

Often, the second paragraph answers the questions a reader has after the lede. If the lede says a strike has started, the second paragraph might say how many workers are involved and why.

## When the first quote comes

Quotes bring a story to life. But where they go matters.

- **Do not start a news story with a quote.** Start with the news. Readers need to know what happened before they hear what someone said about it.
- **Bring in a quote early.** In a story about a speech or an interview, The News Manual advises not to leave the first quote later than the third or fourth paragraph.

A short quote often works well straight after the second paragraph. It lets the reader hear from someone involved while the story is still fresh.

## Ordering the rest

After the first few paragraphs, you have two main choices:

1. **By importance.** Keep going in inverted pyramid order, most important first.
2. **By time.** Tell what happened in the order it happened. This can work for dramatic events.

You can combine them: the most important facts first, then a time-order account below. If you switch to time order, add a short linking paragraph first, such as "The trouble began early on Monday."

## Check before you hand it in

Before you send any story to an editor:

- Read it from the top. Does the lede still say the most important thing?
- Check that you have answered who, what, when, where, why and how, or said what is not yet known.
- Check every name, title, number and date.
