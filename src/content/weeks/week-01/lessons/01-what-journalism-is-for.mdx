## More than information

Anyone with a phone can share information. A WhatsApp voice note, a TikTok video or a tweet can reach thousands of people in minutes. So what makes journalism different?

The answer is in how journalists work and who they work for. Journalism tries to give people accurate information they need to run their lives and hold power to account. To do that, it follows a set of principles.

## The principles most newsrooms share

Different organisations word them differently. The Ethical Journalism Network, an international group of journalists, lists five core principles:

1. **Truth and accuracy.** Get the facts right. The network calls this the "cardinal principle" of journalism.
2. **Independence.** Do not act for a political party, a company or anyone else with an interest in the story.
3. **Fairness and impartiality.** Give people a fair hearing, and tell more than one side where there is more than one side.
4. **Humanity.** Do no harm. Think about how your story affects the people in it.
5. **Accountability.** Own your mistakes, correct them, and listen to your audience.

In their book *The Elements of Journalism*, Bill Kovach and Tom Rosenstiel put it another way. They say journalism's first obligation is to the truth, and its first loyalty is to citizens.

## Verification is the core skill

Kovach and Rosenstiel also say the essence of journalism is "a discipline of <Term id="verification">verification</Term>". This is what separates a news report from a rumour.

Before a journalist publishes, they check. Who said this? How do they know? Is there a document? Can someone else confirm it?

If a claim reaches you on WhatsApp, it is not yet news. It is a tip. Your job is to find out if it is true before you report it. You will learn how in Phase 2.
</Callout>

## Watching those with power

Journalism also acts as an independent monitor of power. That means asking questions of governments, companies and powerful people, and reporting what they do with public money and public trust.

This is why the <Term id="public-interest">public interest</Term> matters so much. A good story is not only one people will click on. It is one that helps people understand what is happening to them.

## Nigeria has its own code

Nigerian journalists have a written code of ethics. The Code of Ethics for Nigerian Journalists has 15 articles. They cover accuracy and fairness, privacy, protecting sources, children, rewards and gifts, the public interest, plagiarism, copyright and press freedom.

Academic sources say the code was adopted in 1998 by the Nigerian press organisations, including the Nigeria Union of Journalists, the Nigerian Guild of Editors and the Nigerian Press Council. The Press Council publishes the full text on its website.

<Callout type="nigeria">
One article, "Reward and Gratification", deals with journalists taking money or gifts to report a story in a certain way. In Nigeria, this is often called the "brown envelope" problem. You will look at it closely in Week 5.
</Callout>
