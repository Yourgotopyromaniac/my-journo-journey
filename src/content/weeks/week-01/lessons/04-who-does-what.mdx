## A team, not one person

A news story may carry one name, the <Term id="byline">byline</Term>. But many people help it reach readers. Knowing these roles helps you understand the job you are aiming for, and who you will work with.

Job titles change from newsroom to newsroom. These are the most common.

## The main roles

**Reporter.** Finds stories, gathers facts, interviews people and writes. A general reporter covers whatever comes up. A beat reporter covers one subject area, such as politics, health or entertainment. That subject area is called a <Term id="beat">beat</Term>.

**Correspondent.** A reporter based in another place, such as another state or another country, who reports from there.

**News editor.** Runs the day's news. Decides which stories to cover, gives stories to reporters, and makes sure they are accurate and on time.

**<Term id="sub-editor">Sub-editor</Term>.** Checks stories before they are published. Fixes errors of fact, grammar and style, trims stories to length and often writes headlines. In some newsrooms this person is called a copy editor.

**Editor.** Leads the newsroom and is responsible for what it publishes. In a large outlet, the top editor may be called the editor-in-chief.

**Producer.** In radio and TV, plans and puts together news programmes.

**Online or social media editor.** Looks after the outlet's website and social media accounts, and decides how stories are shared.

**Fact-checker.** Checks claims made by public figures and organisations. Some outlets have their own fact-checkers. Some organisations, like the ones you met in the last lesson, only fact-check.

## How a story moves through a newsroom

1. **The idea.** A reporter pitches a story, or an editor gives one out.
2. **Reporting.** The reporter gathers facts, finds <Term id="source">sources</Term> and does interviews.
3. **Writing.** The reporter writes the story and sends it in.
4. **Editing.** A sub-editor checks it and may send questions back to the reporter.
5. **Approval.** An editor reads it and approves it for publication.
6. **Publishing.** It goes online, in print or on air, and is shared on social media.
7. **Updates.** If new facts come in, or an error is found, the story is updated or corrected.

<Callout type="practice">
New reporters sometimes feel hurt when a sub-editor changes their story. Try not to. Good editing makes your work better and protects you from mistakes. Read the edited version and learn from the changes.
</Callout>

## Where you might start

Many journalists start as reporters or interns, then move to a beat. Others start in digital roles, such as social media or audience work, and move into reporting. Your two chosen beats, politics and entertainment, both need reporters who are fast, accurate and fair.

## Try it yourself

<Try>
Choose one news story you read today.

1. Who wrote it? Is there a byline?
2. List the people who probably worked on it before it was published.
3. Was it updated or corrected? Look for a note at the top or bottom.
</Try>
