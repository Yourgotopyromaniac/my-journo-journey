## Recording an interview

A recording protects you. It shows exactly what was said, which helps you quote accurately and defend your story if someone later complains.

**Always tell the person you are recording, and ask if they agree, before you start.** Media Helping Media says you must inform a source before recording a call, and that in many places this is a legal requirement.

<Callout type="nigeria" title="What Nigerian law says">
We found no single Nigerian law that clearly says whether you need everyone's permission to record a conversation. The Constitution (Section 37) protects the privacy of telephone conversations. Other laws, including the Cybercrimes Act and the Data Protection Act, cover related issues. Because the law is not clear, the safe and ethical rule is simple: tell people and ask first.
</Callout>

The Code of Ethics for Nigerian Journalists (Article 10) says journalists should use "open and honest means" to gather information. Hidden methods should be used only when the public interest is at stake, and that is a decision for an editor, not a beginner.

## Taking good notes

Even when you record, take notes. Recordings fail, and notes help you find the best quotes quickly.

- Write down key quotes word for word, and mark them.
- Note the time on your recording when something important is said, so you can find it again.
- Before you leave, read through your notes and fill any gaps while you still remember.
- **Type up your notes straight away.** The News Manual gives this advice because notes are hardest to read later.

**Shorthand** is a fast writing system. In the UK, many journalism courses teach a shorthand called Teeline. It is not essential, but some journalists find it useful. If you do not use shorthand, record the interview.

## Keeping notes and recordings safe

Your notes and recordings may contain private information, or details about people who trusted you.

- Keep your tablet and phone locked with a PIN or password.
- Back up recordings somewhere secure.
- Do not leave notebooks where others can read them.
- Keep them for a while after publishing. If someone complains about a story, your notes are your evidence.

## On the record, off the record, on background

Before or during an interview, a source may say "this is off the record". These terms matter, but here is the problem: **they do not mean the same thing to everyone.**

Common meanings are:

    { label: 'On background', text: 'You can use the information, but not name the person. The exact conditions differ between newsrooms.' },

The Associated Press says that because people understand these terms differently, you should **agree the ground rules clearly before the interview.** Poynter adds that the agreement should be made before the person shares the information. A source cannot share something and then decide afterwards it was off the record, unless you agree.

<Callout type="practice">
If someone says "this is off the record", stop and ask: "Do you mean I cannot use this at all, or that I can use it without naming you?" Write down what you agreed.
</Callout>

Article 4 of the Nigerian code says a journalist should not breach an agreement with a source about off-the-record or background information. So only make promises you can keep, and tell your editor about them.
