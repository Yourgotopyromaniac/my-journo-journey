## Types of sources

A <Term id="source">source</Term> is any person, document or record that gives you information. The News Manual describes several kinds:

- **Primary sources** are at the centre of an event: the person who was there, or the official who made the decision.
- **Documents** such as reports, court papers and budgets.
- **Secondary sources** are one step away. The News Manual says most eyewitnesses should be treated as secondary sources, because memory and viewpoint can shape what they report.
- **Experts** who can explain what something means.
- **Anonymous tips.** The News Manual calls these "the most dangerous sources". Always check them.

The Global Investigative Journalism Network (GIJN) adds a warning about whistleblowers: someone who leaks information may have their own reasons. Check what they tell you.

## Building your contacts

The News Manual says it is often said that "a reporter is only as good as his or her contacts book".

Start yours now. Every time someone helps you, save their name, job title, organisation, phone number, and the subject they know about. A simple Google Sheet works well.

**Be fair and independent.** Reuters says sources should be treated with professional politeness and fairness, and journalists should keep a detachment from them. A good test: would you give the same time and attention to someone on the other side of the story?

**Do not pay for information.** Reuters says, "Do not pay for information." Paying a source can make them say what they think you want to hear. Article 7 of the Nigerian code deals with journalists taking bribes to publish or suppress stories. You will study this more in Week 5.

## Interviewing children and people in distress

**Children.** Article 9 of the Nigerian code covers children under 16 involved in cases about sexual offences, crimes, or rituals or witchcraft. It says journalists should not identify them by name or picture, or interview them. This applies whether the child is a victim, a witness or a defendant.

The law goes further. The Child's Rights Act 2003 defines a child as anyone under 18. It says no one should publish anything likely to identify a child whose case is before the court. To be safe, treat everyone under 18 as a child when you decide whether to name or show them.

UNICEF's guidelines for interviewing children start with: do no harm. Make sure the child or guardian knows they are talking to a reporter. Get permission from both the child and their guardian.

**People who have been through trauma.** The Global Center for Journalism and Trauma gives this advice for interviewing victims:

- Agree at the start how the person will be identified.
- Let them choose the time and place of the interview where you can.
- Offer to let a friend or support person be there.
- If something is too painful to talk about, stop.

Media Helping Media adds that sometimes the most ethical choice is not to go ahead with the interview.

## Staying safe

Most interviews are safe. But as a young woman journalist, it is wise to build safe habits from the start. This advice comes from a safety handbook for women journalists by the International Association of Women in Radio and Television (IAWRT):

- **Tell someone where you are going,** who you are meeting, and when you expect to be back.
- **Meet new contacts in a public place,** such as a busy cafe or an office reception, in a well-lit area.
- **Avoid meeting someone in their home.** If you must, take a colleague. If no one can come and you feel unsure, ask for a phone interview or cancel.
- **Trust your instincts.** If something feels wrong, leave.

The Committee to Protect Journalists adds: choose a meeting place with more than one way out, and have a trusted person ready to raise the alarm if needed.
