## A right to ask

The **Freedom of Information (FOI) Act 2011** gives any person the right to ask public institutions for information they hold. It was signed into law on 28 May 2011.

This is one of the most useful laws a journalist has. It lets you get budgets, contracts, reports and records, instead of relying only on what officials choose to say.

## Who it covers

The Act covers public institutions: government ministries, departments and agencies in the executive, legislature and judiciary. It also covers companies the government controls, and private companies that use public funds, provide public services or perform public functions.

<Callout type="nigeria" title="It applies to states too">
For years, courts disagreed about whether the Act applied to state governments. In April 2025, the Supreme Court ruled that the FOI Act applies to all 36 states, not only federal institutions.
</Callout>

## How a request works

**You do not need to give a reason.** The Act says an applicant "needs not demonstrate any specific interest" in the information.

**Put it in writing.** The Act does not set a special form. A clear letter or email is enough. If you ask in person, the official must write your request down and give you a copy.

**The time limit is 7 days.** The institution must give you the information, or refuse in writing with reasons, within 7 days of receiving your request.

- If another institution holds the information, your request can be transferred.
- The institution can extend the time once, by up to 7 more days, if it must search many records or consult others. It must tell you in writing.

**Fees are limited.** Institutions may only charge standard costs for copying and transcribing documents.

## When they refuse, or say nothing

- A refusal must be in writing, give the reason, and say which part of the Act it relies on.
- **Silence counts as a refusal.** If the time limit passes with no answer, the Act treats that as a refusal.
- You can ask a court to review a refusal within **30 days**. The institution must then prove it was allowed to refuse.
- An officer or institution that wrongly refuses can be fined ₦500,000 on conviction.

## What they can hold back

Some information is exempt. Examples include information that could harm defence or international affairs, some law enforcement information, personal information about individuals, trade secrets, and some professional privileges. The list of privileges includes "journalism confidentiality privileges".

Many exemptions have a **public interest** test. If the public interest in releasing the information is stronger than the harm, it should be released. And if only part of a record is exempt, the rest should be released.

Tips:

- Ask for specific documents, not general "information".
- Keep a copy of your request and proof that it was delivered, such as a stamped copy or an email receipt.
- Write down the 7-day deadline and follow up politely.
- Note every reply, delay and refusal. How an institution responds can be part of your story.
