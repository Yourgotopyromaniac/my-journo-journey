## Why ethics matters

In Week 1 you met the core principles of journalism. This week you look closely at how they work in practice, starting with the code Nigerian journalists have written for themselves.

The Code of Ethics for Nigerian Journalists begins with trust:

> "Journalism entails a high degree of public trust. To earn and maintain this trust, it is morally imperative for every journalist and every news medium to observe the highest professional and ethical standards."

It also says: "Truth is the cornerstone of journalism."

## Is the code the law?

No. The code is a set of professional standards, not an Act of the National Assembly. A Premium Times editor told researchers in 2017 that it lacks legal enforcement.

But it still carries weight. Newsrooms use it, and courts can refer to it. In 2024, an FCT High Court ruled in a libel case against a newspaper. It said the paper had breached Article 2(ii), the article on corrections and the right of reply.

## Key articles

The code has 15 articles. These are the ones you will use most often. The quoted words come from the Nigerian Press Council's copy of the code.

**Article 1: Editorial independence.** Decisions about news content "should be the responsibility of a professional journalist". Not an advertiser, a politician or a friend.

**Article 2: Accuracy and fairness.** Journalists should not publish "inaccurate and misleading information". If a mistake is published, "prompt correction should be made". And the code calls the **right of reply** a "cardinal rule": people you report on should be able to respond. It also asks journalists to separate facts from "conjecture and comment".

**Article 3: Privacy.** Respect people's privacy "unless it affects public interest". Publishing private information is justified only when it is aimed at:

- exposing crime or serious misconduct
- exposing anti-social conduct
- protecting public health, morality and safety
- stopping the public from being misled by what someone has said or done

**Article 5: Decency.** Avoid "lurid details" of violence or sexual acts. In cases of grief or shock, approach people "with sympathy and discretion". Generally avoid naming relatives or friends of people accused or convicted of crimes, unless the public's right to know requires it.

**Article 6: Discrimination.** Do not make negative references to a person's "ethnic group, religion, sex" or any physical or mental illness or disability.

**Article 7: Reward and gratification.** Do not ask for or accept a bribe, gratification or patronage "to suppress or publish information". You will look at this closely in the next lesson.

**Article 11: Public interest.** Journalists should "strive to enhance national unity and public good".

**Article 13: Plagiarism.** Do not copy other people's work, whole or in part, "without attribution and/or consent".

<Callout type="practice">
When you face a hard choice, ask three questions. Is it true and fair? Does it serve the public interest? Could it cause harm that the story does not justify?
</Callout>

## Newsroom codes

Many outlets also have their own editorial policies. They usually add detail, such as rules on gifts, corrections and unnamed sources. When you join a newsroom, read its policy on your first day.
