## Reporting without causing needless harm

Journalism often involves people on their worst days. The Ethical Journalism Network's principle of humanity asks journalists to "do no harm". That does not mean avoiding hard stories. It means thinking about how your story affects the people in it, and not causing harm the story does not justify.

## Grief and shock

Article 5 of the Nigerian code says that in cases of personal grief or shock, enquiries should be made "with sympathy and discretion".

- Introduce yourself clearly and explain why you are there.
- Accept a "no". Leave your contact details in case they change their mind.
- Do not push for graphic details.

Article 5 also says journalists should generally avoid identifying relatives or friends of people accused or convicted of crime, unless the public's right to know requires it.

## Children

You met the rules in Week 4. In short: Article 9 of the code protects children under 16 in certain cases, and the Child's Rights Act protects all under-18s whose cases are before a court. When in doubt, do not identify a child.

## Survivors of sexual violence

The Centre for Journalism Innovation and Development (CJID) has published a handbook for Nigerian journalists on reporting gender-based violence. It says:

- Reporting should protect the safety, confidentiality and dignity of survivors and their families.
- Survivors should not be identified or made identifiable. Think about details like the street, school or workplace, not only the name.
- Survivors can change their minds and withdraw consent at any stage, without giving a reason.
- Consent should be clear, in a language the survivor understands.
- Where possible, a woman should interview a woman survivor.

## Ethnicity and religion

Article 6 of the code says journalists should not make negative references to a person's ethnic group or religion. In Nigeria, careless words about ethnicity or religion can inflame tension, especially during elections or conflicts.

- **Be careful with labels.** GIJN warns that labels such as "Fulani herders" or "Christian indigenes" can harden into stereotypes. HumAngle's editorial policy avoids ethnic and religious labels completely.
- **Only mention ethnicity or religion when it is truly relevant** to understanding the story.
- **Be careful with casualty figures** in violent conflicts. Check them, and say where they came from.

**When someone says something inflammatory.** The Ethical Journalism Network has a five-point test to help decide whether and how to report hateful speech. It asks you to consider:

1. the position or status of the speaker
2. how far the speech could reach
3. what the speaker is trying to achieve
4. the content and form of the speech
5. the economic, social and political climate

As the network puts it: "just because someone says something outrageous that does not make it news."
