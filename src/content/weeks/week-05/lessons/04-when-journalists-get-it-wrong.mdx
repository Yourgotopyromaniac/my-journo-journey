## Everyone makes mistakes

Every journalist gets something wrong eventually. What matters is what you do next. The Nigerian code says that when inaccurate information is published by mistake, "prompt correction should be made".

## How corrections work

Many newsrooms publish correction policies. TheCable's policy, for example, says that when an error is confirmed, it updates the story and adds an editor's note explaining what was corrected and why. For stories that are badly flawed, it may publish a clearly labelled retraction.

Peoples Gazette's policy says it will retract stories found to be false, and publish an explanation or apology in a prominent place once an error is admitted.

## Writing a correction

Craig Silverman, writing for the American Press Institute, gives clear advice:

- **Say exactly what was wrong, and what is right.**
- **Call it a correction.** Do not hide it as an "update" or a "note".
- **Give it the same prominence** as the original mistake.
- **Do not write a foggy correction** that confuses readers more.

## Case study: one source, no checks

In November 2014, the US magazine Rolling Stone published a story about a sexual assault at the University of Virginia. It was based mainly on one person's account, and key parts of it were not checked.

Police later found no substantive basis for the account, and the magazine withdrew the story. In April 2015, a review by the Columbia Journalism School called it "a story of journalistic failure that was avoidable". It found failures in reporting, editing, supervision and fact-checking. In 2016, a jury found Rolling Stone liable for defaming a university official and awarded $3 million in damages.

The review also noted that withdrawing the story does not show what did or did not happen to the woman at its centre.

**Lessons:** a sensitive subject makes careful checking more important, not less. Do not rely on one unchecked source for a serious claim.

## Case study: headlines that went too far

In 2018, the editor of South Africa's Sunday Times published a public apology for a series of stories. He wrote that the paper had "allowed ourselves to be manipulated by those with ulterior motives". He also admitted its headlines had "overstated the contents of the reports" the stories were based on. The paper returned awards it had won for the stories.

**Lessons:** ask why a source is giving you information. Make sure headlines match the facts. Include information that does not fit your story's angle.

## Owning your mistakes

<Callout type="practice">
If you find an error in your own story, tell your editor straight away. Do not quietly change it and hope no one notices. Readers trust journalists who admit and fix mistakes.
