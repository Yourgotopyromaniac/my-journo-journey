## What a brown envelope is

In Nigerian journalism, a "brown envelope" means money or gifts given to journalists to cover a story, or to cover it in a certain way. It is often cash handed out at press conferences and described as "transport money" or money for fuel. It can also take other forms, such as gifts, trips or other favours.

Nigerian journalists have many slang words for it, including "egunje", "kola", "transport" and "Ghana Must Go".

Article 7 of the code is clear: a journalist should not solicit or accept a bribe, gratification or patronage "to suppress or publish information".

## How common is it?

Researchers have studied this, but the studies are small, local and mostly old. Treat their numbers as a snapshot, not a national figure.

- A 2009 study of 184 journalists in Lagos found that 61% said they regularly received brown envelopes on assignments.
- A 2010 survey of 116 journalists in five cities found that 70% described the envelope as "a gift or kind gesture". Only 8% called it a bribe.
- In a 2026 survey of 50 people who had left journalism, 14% said they had accepted brown envelopes to get by.

<Callout type="watch" title="Be careful with these numbers">
You may see "61% of Nigerian journalists take brown envelopes" quoted as fact. That figure comes from one 2009 study of 184 journalists in Lagos. It does not describe all Nigerian journalists today. This is a good example of checking where a number comes from.
</Callout>

## Why it happens

The reasons are debated.

**Low and late pay.** A 2017 survey of 204 journalists, reported by Premium Times, found 37.2% were not paid regularly. Journalists and union leaders have linked poor and unpaid salaries to brown envelopes. A 2015 Ethical Journalism Network report quoted concern that some media owners see a journalist's ID card as a "meal ticket" that takes the place of a salary.

**But pay is not the whole story.** In the 2010 survey, only 43% said poor pay was the basic reason. Only 4% believed no journalist would take envelopes if salaries rose. Culture, training and pressure from sources all play a part.

## Why it harms journalism

A journalist who takes money from a source owes that source something. Even if you write the same story, readers cannot tell whether it was paid for. And sources learn that coverage can be bought, which hurts every honest journalist.

## What newsrooms do

Some Nigerian newsrooms have written policies. Peoples Gazette's editorial policy, for example:

- bans journalists from accepting brown envelopes from news sources
- says the outlet will not demand or receive payment for news coverage
- allows gifts only up to ₦10,000 in value, and never cash
- asks journalists to declare to an editor any gift that cannot be refused without causing offence

## Conflicts of interest

A **conflict of interest** is anything that could affect, or look like it affects, your fairness. The BBC defines it as a risk that a person's choices outside work could affect, or be "reasonably perceived as affecting", their impartiality, independence and integrity.

Common examples:

- gifts, free trips or special treatment from people you report on
- reporting on a company, party or cause you are part of or work for
- doing paid PR work for someone you might later cover

The US Society of Professional Journalists code puts it simply: "Avoid conflicts of interest, real or perceived. Disclose unavoidable conflicts."

<Callout type="nigeria" title="Entertainment journalism and brand deals">
Entertainment journalists are often offered free concert tickets, clothes, products or paid posts. The BBC does not allow its presenters to accept free products on the understanding that they will use or mention them on air. Many journalists also build personal brands on social media. If a brand pays you, you cannot fairly review or report on that brand. Say no, or tell your editor and your audience.
</Callout>

## What to do when offered money or gifts

1. **Say no politely.** "Thank you, but I can't accept this. It's my newsroom's policy."
2. **If refusing would cause real offence,** accept only something small and not cash, and tell your editor.
3. **Tell your editor** about any offer, especially from someone in a story.
4. **Write the story the same way** you would have without the offer.
