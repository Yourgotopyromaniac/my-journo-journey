# Digital Journalism Learning Roadmap
## Beginner Level | 26 Weeks

---

> **How to Use This Roadmap**
> This roadmap is designed for complete beginners with little to no prior experience in digital journalism. Each week builds on the last, gradually introducing new skills while reinforcing previous ones. Expect to dedicate approximately **8–12 hours per week** to study and practice. Free tools are prioritized throughout, with paid alternatives noted where relevant.

---

## 📋 PHASE OVERVIEW

| Phase | Weeks | Focus Area |
|-------|-------|------------|
| **Phase 1: Foundations** | 1–6 | Journalism basics, writing, and digital literacy |
| **Phase 2: Core Digital Skills** | 7–13 | Multimedia, social media, and content management |
| **Phase 3: Advanced Storytelling** | 14–19 | Data journalism, video, audio, and interactives |
| **Phase 4: Professional Practice** | 20–26 | Ethics, audience growth, portfolio, and career launch |

---

## PHASE 1: FOUNDATIONS
### *Weeks 1–6 | Building Your Journalistic Base*

---

### Week 1
**Introduction to Journalism and the Digital Landscape**

---

#### 📚 Topics to Learn
- What is journalism? Traditional vs. digital journalism
- The role of a digital journalist in today's media ecosystem
- Key journalism principles: accuracy, fairness, independence, accountability
- Overview of major digital news outlets (The Guardian, BuzzFeed News, Vice, local digital outlets)
- Understanding your audience: who reads digital news and why
- Introduction to the news cycle in a digital-first world

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
By the end of this week, you should be able to:
- [ ] Define digital journalism and explain how it differs from print or broadcast journalism
- [ ] Name and describe at least five core journalistic principles
- [ ] Identify at least five major digital news organizations and describe their editorial focus
- [ ] Explain what the 24-hour digital news cycle means and how it affects reporting

#### 🛠️ Practical Exercises
1. **Media Audit**: Spend one hour reading news from five different digital outlets (e.g., BBC News online, The Atlantic, a local digital news site, a newsletter like The Skimm, and a social media news account). Write a 300-word reflection comparing their tone, format, and audience.
2. **Outlet Analysis**: Choose one digital news outlet and answer: Who is their audience? What topics do they cover? How do they publish (articles, videos, podcasts)? Present your findings in a simple one-page document.
3. **News Diary**: Start a daily news diary this week. Each day, note three news stories you encountered, where you found them, and why they caught your attention. You will continue this diary throughout the course.

---

### Week 2
**The Fundamentals of News Writing**

---

#### 📚 Topics to Learn
- The inverted pyramid structure: what it is and why it matters
- The Five W's and One H: Who, What, When, Where, Why, How
- Writing a strong news lead (lede)
- Hard news vs. soft news vs. features
- News values: timeliness, proximity, prominence, conflict, human interest, novelty
- Basic grammar and style for journalism (AP Style introduction)

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
By the end of this week, you should be able to:
- [ ] Write a clear, accurate news lead using the inverted pyramid structure
- [ ] Identify the Five W's and One H in any given news story
- [ ] Distinguish between hard news, soft news, and feature writing
- [ ] Apply at least four news values when evaluating whether a story is newsworthy
- [ ] Demonstrate basic AP Style usage (capitalization, numbers, abbreviations)

#### 🛠️ Practical Exercises
1. **Lead Writing Drill**: Find five news stories online. Cover the byline and lead, then write your own lead for each story based only on the headline. Compare your lead to the original and note differences.
2. **Inverted Pyramid Rewrite**: Take a 500-word news article and rewrite it in 200 words using the inverted pyramid structure. Focus on keeping the most important information at the top.
3. **Newsworthiness Checklist**: Find 10 stories from your news diary. Rate each one on a scale of 1–5 for each of the six news values. Which stories score highest overall? Write a brief explanation of your ratings.

---

### Week 3
**Interviewing Skills and Source Development**

---

#### 📚 Topics to Learn
- Why interviews are the backbone of journalism
- Types of interviews: in-person, phone, email, video call
- Preparing for an interview: research, question development
- Open-ended vs. closed questions
- Active listening techniques
- Note-taking methods: shorthand basics, recording etiquette
- On the record, off the record, on background, and deep background
- Building and maintaining a source network

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
By the end of this week, you should be able to:
- [ ] Prepare a structured interview question list for a given topic
- [ ] Conduct a basic interview using open-ended questions
- [ ] Accurately transcribe key quotes from an interview
- [ ] Explain the difference between on-the-record and off-the-record conversations
- [ ] Describe three strategies for building a reliable source network

#### 🛠️ Practical Exercises
1. **Practice Interview**: Interview a friend, family member, or classmate about a topic they know well (their job, a hobby, a recent experience). Prepare at least 10 questions in advance. Record the interview (with permission) and transcribe three usable quotes.
2. **Question Audit**: Find a published Q&A interview online. Analyze the questions asked: How many are open-ended? How many are closed? How does the journalist follow up? Write a 200-word critique.
3. **Source Map**: Create a simple source map for a hypothetical story about "rising food prices in your city." List at least eight types of sources you would contact (e.g., economists, grocery store owners, food bank workers, local government officials) and explain why each is valuable.

---

### Week 4
**Research, Verification, and Fact-Checking**

---

#### 📚 Topics to Learn
- Why verification is the cornerstone of credible journalism
- Primary vs. secondary sources
- How to evaluate source credibility online
- Introduction to fact-checking organizations (Snopes, PolitiFact, FactCheck.org, Full Fact)
- Reverse image search and photo verification (Google Images, TinEye)
- Checking social media claims: tools and techniques
- Introduction to public records and Freedom of Information (FOI) requests
- Avoiding misinformation and disinformation in your reporting

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
By the end of this week, you should be able to:
- [ ] Distinguish between primary and secondary sources and explain when to use each
- [ ] Use at least three online tools to verify a claim or image
- [ ] Identify red flags that suggest a source or story may be unreliable
- [ ] Explain what a Freedom of Information request is and when a journalist would use one
- [ ] Fact-check a simple news claim using publicly available information

#### 🛠️ Practical Exercises
1. **Fact-Check Challenge**: Choose three viral social media posts or claims from the past month. Use fact-checking tools (Snopes, Google, reverse image search) to verify or debunk each one. Write a 150-word fact-check summary for each.
2. **Source Credibility Audit**: Find five websites that publish news or information. Evaluate each using the SIFT method (Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace claims). Rate each site's credibility and explain your reasoning.
3. **FOI Simulation**: Research the FOI process in your country. Draft a sample FOI request letter asking a local government body for information about public spending on a specific issue. You do not need to send it — this is a drafting exercise.

---

### Week 5
**Digital Writing: Style, Tone, and Online Readability**

---

#### 📚 Topics to Learn
- How online reading differs from print reading (F-pattern, scanning behavior)
- Writing for the web: short paragraphs, subheadings, bullet points
- Headlines for digital: clarity, keywords, and avoiding clickbait
- Introduction to SEO (Search Engine Optimization) basics for journalists
- Writing for different digital formats: breaking news, explainers, listicles, Q&As
- The importance of linking: internal and external links in digital articles
- Accessibility in writing: plain language, readability scores

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
By the end of this week, you should be able to:
- [ ] Reformat a print-style article for digital readability
- [ ] Write three different headline styles for the same story (SEO-focused, social-friendly, print-style)
- [ ] Explain what SEO means and identify three ways it affects how journalists write headlines and articles
- [ ] Produce a short explainer article (400–500 words) on a topic of your choice
- [ ] Use a readability tool to assess and improve the clarity of your writing

#### 🛠️ Practical Exercises
1. **Digital Reformat**: Take a 600-word print-style news article (from a newspaper archive or your own writing). Reformat it for digital: add subheadings, break up paragraphs, add a pull quote, and rewrite the headline for SEO. Compare the two versions.
2. **Headline Workshop**: Choose five news stories. Write three different headlines for each: one optimized for SEO, one for social media sharing, and one in traditional print style. Reflect on which approach works best for each story.
3. **Explainer Article**: Write a 400–500 word explainer on a topic you know well (e.g., "How does your local council work?" or "What is climate change?"). Use subheadings, bullet points, and plain language. Run it through the Hemingway App (free online tool) and aim for a Grade 8 reading level or below.

---

### Week 6
**Introduction to Content Management Systems (CMS)**

---

#### 📚 Topics to Learn
- What is a CMS and why do journalists use them?
- Overview of major CMS platforms: WordPress, Ghost, Squarespace, Medium
- Setting up a free WordPress.com or Ghost account
- Basic CMS navigation: dashboard, posts, pages, categories, tags
- Formatting a post: headings, bold/italic, links, images
- Publishing workflow: draft, review, publish, schedule
- Introduction to metadata: titles, descriptions, slugs, and alt text
- Basic image sourcing: copyright-free images (Unsplash, Pexels, Wikimedia Commons)

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
By the end of this week, you should be able to:
- [ ] Set up and navigate a basic CMS account (WordPress or Ghost)
- [ ] Publish a properly formatted article including headings, links, and an image
- [ ] Explain what metadata is and correctly fill in a post's SEO title, description, and slug
- [ ] Source and correctly attribute a copyright-free image for use in an article
- [ ] Describe the standard publishing workflow used in most digital newsrooms

#### 🛠️ Practical Exercises
1. **CMS Setup**: Create a free WordPress.com or Ghost account. Set up a basic site with a name, tagline, and "About" page. Explore every section of the dashboard and write a brief note about what each section does.
2. **First Published Article**: Take the explainer article you wrote in Week 5 and publish it on your new CMS. Include: a formatted headline, at least two subheadings, one copyright-free image with proper attribution, one internal link (to your About page), and one external link. Fill in the SEO metadata fields.
3. **CMS Comparison**: Sign up for a free Medium account as well. Publish the same article on Medium. Compare the two platforms: Which is easier to use? Which looks more professional? Which has better built-in audience features? Write a 200-word comparison.

## PHASE 2: CORE DIGITAL SKILLS
### *Weeks 7–13 | Multimedia, Social Media, and Newsroom Tools*

---

### Week 7
**Photography for Digital Journalism**

---

#### 📚 Topics to Learn
- The role of photography in digital storytelling
- Basic photography composition: rule of thirds, leading lines, framing
- Shooting news photos vs. feature photos
- Smartphone photography tips for journalists
- Photo editing basics: cropping, brightness, contrast (no heavy manipulation)
- Ethical rules in photojournalism: what you can and cannot edit
- Photo captions: how to write accurate, informative captions
- Image file formats: JPEG, PNG, WebP — when to use each

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
By the end of this week, you should be able to:
- [ ] Apply at least three composition techniques when taking news photographs
- [ ] Edit a photo appropriately using a free tool (Snapseed, Lightroom Mobile, or GIMP)
- [ ] Write a complete, accurate photo caption following journalism standards
- [ ] Explain the ethical boundaries of photo editing in journalism
- [ ] Optimize an image for web use (correct file size and format)

#### 🛠️ Practical Exercises
1. **Photo Walk**: Go outside and take 20 photographs of your local area with a journalistic purpose in mind (document something happening in your community). Apply at least three composition techniques. Select your best five and write a caption for each.
2. **Photo Edit Practice**: Take three of your photos and edit them using Snapseed (free mobile app) or GIMP (free desktop). Adjust only brightness, contrast, and crop. Write a brief note explaining each edit and why it was ethically acceptable.
3. **Photo Story**: Create a five-image photo story about a single subject or event (e.g., a local market, a community event, a person's daily routine). Write a 100-word introduction and a caption for each image. Publish it on your CMS from Week 6.

---

### Week 8
**Social Media for Journalists**

---

#### 📚 Topics to Learn
- Social media as a newsgathering and distribution tool
- Platform overview: X (Twitter), Instagram, Facebook, LinkedIn, TikTok, Threads
- Building a professional journalist profile on social media
- Live tweeting/posting a news event
- Using social media for source finding and story ideas
- Verifying social media content: checking accounts, cross-referencing
- Social media ethics: what journalists should and should not post
- Understanding algorithms: how content gets seen on each platform
- Introduction to social media analytics

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
By the end of this week, you should be able to:
- [ ] Set up a professional journalist profile on at least two social media platforms
- [ ] Demonstrate how to live-post a news event in real time
- [ ] Use social media search tools to find sources and story ideas
- [ ] Apply a verification checklist to a social media claim or account
- [ ] Explain how platform algorithms affect news distribution

#### 🛠️ Practical Exercises
1. **Profile Audit**: Set up or update your professional profiles on X (Twitter) and LinkedIn. Write a professional bio for each (they should be tailored to each platform's tone). Follow at least 20 journalists, editors, and news organizations in your area of interest.
2. **Live Coverage Simulation**: Watch a live event (a press conference, a sports event, a public meeting — even on YouTube) and live-tweet or live-post it on X or Threads. Aim for at least 10 posts in real time. Review your posts afterward: Were they accurate? Timely? Engaging?
3. **Social Media Story Hunt**: Spend 30 minutes on social media specifically looking for potential news stories in your local area or area of interest. Find at least three story ideas. For each, identify: the potential story angle, at least two sources you could contact, and how you would verify the initial claim.

### Week 9
**Audio Journalism and Podcasting Basics**

---

#### 📚 Topics to Learn
- The rise of audio journalism and podcasting
- Types of audio journalism: news podcasts, narrative podcasts, radio-style reports
- Basic audio recording equipment: smartphone microphones, lapel mics, USB mics
- Recording techniques: room acoustics, reducing background noise, mic placement
- Introduction to audio editing software: Audacity (free), GarageBand (Mac/iOS)
- Basic audio editing: cutting, fading, noise reduction, leveling
- Writing for the ear vs. writing for the eye
- Structuring a short audio report or podcast episode
- Hosting platforms: Anchor (Spotify for Podcasters), SoundCloud, Buzzsprout

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
By the end of this week, you should be able to:
- [ ] Record a clean audio clip using a smartphone or basic microphone
- [ ] Edit an audio file in Audacity or GarageBand (cut, fade, noise reduction)
- [ ] Write a script designed specifically for audio delivery
- [ ] Produce a short (3–5 minute) audio news report or podcast segment
- [ ] Upload and publish audio content to a free hosting platform

#### 🛠️ Practical Exercises
1. **Audio Recording Test**: Record yourself reading a 200-word news script in three different locations (e.g., outdoors, in a quiet room, in a room with background noise). Compare the audio quality. Write notes on what affected the sound and how you would improve it.
2. **Audio Edit Practice**: Download a free audio clip from Freesound.org or record a 5-minute conversation. Import it into Audacity. Practice: cutting out a 30-second section, adding a fade in/out, reducing background noise, and exporting as an MP3.
3. **Mini Podcast Episode**: Produce a 3–5 minute audio piece on a topic from your news diary. Include: a scripted introduction, at least one recorded interview clip (or a recorded quote read aloud), and a scripted conclusion. Edit it in Audacity and publish it on Spotify for Podcasters (free).

### Week 10
**Video Journalism Fundamentals**

---

#### 📚 Topics to Learn
- The role of video in digital journalism
- Types of video journalism: news packages, explainer videos, documentary shorts, social video
- Smartphone video journalism: settings, stabilization, framing
- Basic video composition: rule of thirds, headroom, lead room
- Shooting an interview on video: setup, lighting, background
- Introduction to video editing: CapCut (free, mobile), DaVinci Resolve (free, desktop)
- Basic editing workflow: importing, cutting, sequencing, adding text
- Writing video scripts and narration
- Vertical vs. horizontal video: when to use each format

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
By the end of this week, you should be able to:
- [ ] Record a properly framed and stable video interview using a smartphone
- [ ] Apply basic video composition rules in your footage
- [ ] Edit a short video (1–3 minutes) using CapCut or DaVinci Resolve
- [ ] Add text overlays, captions, and a simple title card to a video
- [ ] Explain the difference between video formats for different platforms (YouTube, Instagram, TikTok)

#### 🛠️ Practical Exercises
1. **Composition Practice**: Record 10 short video clips (15–30 seconds each) practicing different composition techniques: rule of thirds, close-up, wide shot, over-the-shoulder, and cutaway shots. Review them and note which look most professional.
2. **Video Interview**: Film a 3–5 minute interview with a friend or family member on any topic. Focus on: proper framing, stable footage, good lighting (near a window), and clear audio. Edit it down to the best 90 seconds using CapCut or DaVinci Resolve.
3. **News Video Package**: Produce a 2–3 minute video news package on a local topic or community issue. Include: a scripted stand-up introduction (you on camera), interview clips, B-roll footage, and text captions. Publish it on YouTube (you can set it to unlisted if you prefer privacy) and embed it in a post on your CMS.

### Week 11
**Data Literacy for Journalists**

---

#### 📚 Topics to Learn
- Why data matters in journalism: data-driven storytelling
- Types of data journalists use: government data, surveys, statistics, databases
- Reading and interpreting basic statistics: percentages, averages, rates
- Common statistical mistakes journalists make (and how to avoid them)
- Introduction to spreadsheets: Google Sheets basics
- Finding data: government open data portals, data.gov, ONS, Eurostat, World Bank
- Cleaning data: removing duplicates, fixing formatting, handling missing values
- Basic data visualization concepts: when to use charts, tables, and maps

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
By the end of this week, you should be able to:
- [ ] Find and download a dataset from a public government data portal
- [ ] Perform basic calculations in Google Sheets (SUM, AVERAGE, percentage change)
- [ ] Identify at least three common statistical errors in news reporting
- [ ] Explain what data cleaning is and perform basic cleaning tasks in Google Sheets
- [ ] Describe when a chart, table, or map is the most appropriate way to present data

#### 🛠️ Practical Exercises
1. **Data Hunt**: Visit three public data portals (e.g., data.gov, your national statistics office, or the World Bank Open Data portal). Find one interesting dataset in each. Write a 100-word summary of each dataset: What does it measure? Who collected it? When was it last updated? What story could it tell?
2. **Spreadsheet Basics**: Download a simple dataset (e.g., population data by country from the World Bank). Import it into Google Sheets. Calculate: the total, the average, the highest and lowest values, and the percentage change between two years. Format it neatly with headers and bold totals.
3. **Spot the Error**: Find five news articles that use statistics or data. For each, identify: the original data source (is it cited?), whether the statistics are used correctly, and any potential errors or misleading framing. Write a brief critique of each.

**Data Visualization and Infographics**

---

#### 📚 Topics to Learn
- Principles of good data visualization: clarity, accuracy, simplicity
- Chart types and when to use them: bar charts, line charts, pie charts, scatter plots
- Introduction to Datawrapper (free, browser-based chart tool for journalists)
- Introduction to Canva for infographic creation (free tier)
- Color theory basics for data visualization: accessibility, contrast
- Embedding charts and graphics into CMS articles

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
By the end of this week, you should be able to:
- [ ] Create at least three different chart types in Datawrapper using real data
- [ ] Design a simple infographic in Canva that accurately represents a data story
- [ ] Apply basic color accessibility principles to a visualization
- [ ] Embed a Datawrapper chart into a CMS article
- [ ] Critique a published data visualization for accuracy and clarity

#### 🛠️ Practical Exercises
1. **Datawrapper Charts**: Using the dataset you found in Week 11, create three different charts in Datawrapper: a bar chart, a line chart, and one of your choice. Add proper titles, source citations, and labels to each. Export or embed them.
2. **Infographic Design**: Using Canva, design a one-page infographic explaining a simple data story (e.g., "How has the cost of living changed in the last 5 years?" or "The most popular social media platforms by age group"). Use real data and cite your sources.
3. **Data Story Article**: Write a 500-word data-driven article using the dataset from Week 11. Embed at least two Datawrapper charts. Publish it on your CMS. This is your first data journalism piece — save it for your portfolio.

### Week 13
**Newsletter Journalism and Email Publishing**

---

#### 📚 Topics to Learn
- The newsletter renaissance: why email journalism is thriving
- Types of journalism newsletters: daily briefings, niche newsletters, investigative digests
- Introduction to newsletter platforms: Substack (free), Mailchimp (free tier), Beehiiv
- Newsletter structure: subject line, preview text, introduction, body, call to action
- Writing for email: tone, length, formatting
- Building and growing a subscriber list
- Newsletter analytics: open rates, click rates, unsubscribes
- Monetization basics: free vs. paid newsletters

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
By the end of this week, you should be able to:
- [ ] Set up a newsletter on Substack or Mailchimp
- [ ] Write and send a properly formatted newsletter issue
- [ ] Explain what open rate and click rate mean and what good benchmarks look like
- [ ] Describe at least three strategies for growing a newsletter subscriber list
- [ ] Identify the key differences between newsletter journalism and traditional article publishing

#### 🛠️ Practical Exercises
1. **Newsletter Setup**: Create a free Substack account. Set up your newsletter with a name, description, logo (use Canva), and welcome email. Define your newsletter's niche: What topic will it cover? Who is your target reader?
2. **First Newsletter Issue**: Write and publish your first newsletter issue (400–600 words). Include: a compelling subject line, a brief introduction, one main story or analysis, two or three curated links with brief commentary, and a closing call to action (e.g., "Reply and tell me what you think").
3. **Newsletter Audit**: Subscribe to five journalism newsletters in your area of interest (many are free). Over the week, analyze each one: What is their format? How long are they? What is their tone? What makes you want to keep reading? Write a 300-word analysis of what makes a great journalism newsletter.

### Week 14
**Investigative Journalism Fundamentals**

---

#### 📚 Topics to Learn
- What is investigative journalism? How it differs from daily reporting
- The investigative process: tip, research, document gathering, interviews, verification, publication
- Document-based reporting: court records, company filings, government documents
- Introduction to OSINT (Open Source Intelligence) for journalists
- Key OSINT tools: Google advanced search operators, LinkedIn, Companies House, PACER
- Building a document trail and organizing evidence
- Protecting sources: digital security basics for journalists
- Notable investigative journalism examples: Panama Papers, Watergate, Cambridge Analytica

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
By the end of this week, you should be able to:
- [ ] Describe the investigative journalism process from tip to publication
- [ ] Use Google advanced search operators to find specific documents or information
- [ ] Identify at least five public records databases relevant to your country
- [ ] Explain what OSINT is and demonstrate three OSINT techniques
- [ ] Describe basic digital security practices journalists use to protect sources

#### 🛠️ Practical Exercises
1. **OSINT Challenge**: Choose a local business or public figure. Using only publicly available information (company registries, LinkedIn, news archives, public records), compile a factual profile. Note every source you used. This exercise is about research skills — treat the subject ethically and do not publish.
2. **Advanced Search Drill**: Practice Google advanced search operators. Use `site:`, `filetype:`, `"exact phrase"`, and date range filters to find: a government PDF report, a specific press release, and a news article from more than five years ago on a topic of your choice. Document your search strings.
3. **Case Study Analysis**: Read about one major investigative journalism project (e.g., the Panama Papers on ICIJ.org, or a ProPublica investigation). Write a 400-word breakdown: What was the story? How did journalists find the information? What documents or data did they use? What was the impact?

### Week 15
**Long-Form and Feature Writing**

---

#### 📚 Topics to Learn
- What is long-form journalism? Narrative non-fiction, profiles, in-depth features
- Structure of a long-form piece: nut graf, scene-setting, narrative arc
- Scene-setting and descriptive writing techniques
- Character development in journalism: writing about real people compellingly
- The art of the profile: researching and writing about a person
- Pacing and transitions in long-form writing
- Editing long-form work: self-editing strategies
- Examples of excellent long-form digital journalism (The Atavist, Longform.org, The New Yorker online)

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
By the end of this week, you should be able to:
- [ ] Identify and write a strong "nut graf" (the paragraph that explains why a story matters)
- [ ] Use scene-setting and descriptive language to open a feature story
- [ ] Structure a 1,000-word feature article with a clear narrative arc
- [ ] Write a compelling 600-word profile of a real person
- [ ] Apply self-editing techniques to improve a draft by at least 20%

#### 🛠️ Practical Exercises
1. **Nut Graf Practice**: Find five long-form articles online. Identify the nut graf in each. Then take three news stories from your news diary and write a nut graf for each, explaining why the story matters to the reader.
2. **Scene-Setting Exercise**: Write a 300-word scene-setting opening for a feature story. Choose a real location you know well (a market, a school, a neighborhood). Use sensory details (sight, sound, smell) to put the reader in the scene without using the word "I."
3. **Profile Article**: Interview someone in your community (a local business owner, a teacher, a community organizer) and write a 600–800 word profile. Include: a scene-setting opening, a nut graf, at least three direct quotes, background context, and a strong closing line. Publish it on your CMS.

#### 🛠️ Practical Exercises

2. **Story Map**: Using Google My Maps, create a map-based story about your local area. Plot at least five locations with descriptions, photos, and links. This could be a map of local community organizations, historical sites, or locations relevant to a news story.
3. **Multimedia Story**: Produce a multimedia article combining: a 400-word written piece, one embedded video (from Week 10 or new footage), one Datawrapper chart (from Week 12), and one embedded map (from this week's exercise). Publish it on your CMS. This is a major portfolio piece.

### Week 17
**Audience Analytics and Digital Metrics**

---

#### 📚 Topics to Learn
- Why analytics matter in digital journalism
- Key metrics: pageviews, unique visitors, time on page, bounce rate, scroll depth
- Introduction to Google Analytics 4 (GA4): setup and navigation
- Social media analytics: reach, impressions, engagement rate, follower growth
- Understanding referral traffic: where your readers come from
- A/B testing headlines and content
- Using analytics to inform editorial decisions (without compromising editorial integrity)
- Audience personas: who is reading your work and why

#### 🎯 Learning Objectives
By the end of this week, you should be able to:

- [ ] Navigate the GA4 dashboard and interpret basic traffic reports
- [ ] Define and explain at least six key digital metrics
- [ ] Analyze the social media performance of three of your published posts
